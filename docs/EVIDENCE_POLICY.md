# Evidence policy and review

## Assertions should say what is known, and how

Prefer “the inscription records X,” “the excavators assign this phase to Y,” or “the project’s final report interprets the text as Z” when the evidence warrants an attributed statement rather than a universal fact. Do not convert a source’s boast, allegation, retrospective genealogy or hypothesis into an unqualified event.

The oldest local occupation, earliest writing, oldest rock and first appearance of a practice are especially demanding rankings. A source saying “first known” in one publication year does not make that a permanently current result. Ranking requires a defined corpus, the date of its review, geographical eligibility and comparable criteria.

## Sources are not all interchangeable

A primary document can be incomplete, partisan or mistaken. A modern critical edition can improve access while adding editorial interpretations. An official tourism page is primary evidence for a tourism claim, not a geochronological measurement. An institutional retrospective can locate earlier publications but is not those publications. A catalogue or abstract can justify a discovery lead, not a claim about unseen pages.

Read the relevant continuous passage and inspect a page image when parsing is ambiguous. Verify tables, inscriptions, marginalia and map legends in the image rather than trusting OCR. For scripts requiring specialist knowledge, preserve the image locator and state that the reading remains unverified; machine translation is not an epigraphic edition.

## Corrections and versions

Search for errata, corrigenda, retractions, new editions, alternative readings and responses. Link them before using disputed numerical results. On S020, the research proposal and final outcomes on the same page describe the Kollam plates differently. Record the passage and stage used. An earlier proposal is not the final result. ## Independent evidence

Three copied webpages do not provide three independent confirmations. Record underlying dependencies. A translated traveller’s account and a later book quoting that account are one evidentiary chain. A separately dated archaeological context may provide another, but only for the particular claim it supports.

For high-impact claims, seek the primary witness or measurement, a critical specialist evaluation, and independent corroboration where the subject permits. Where corroboration does not exist, retain the solitary witness and say so. Never manufacture a two-source rule that hides rare records or promotes poor duplicated sources.

## Disagreement

Keep incompatible readings, date ranges and place identifications as separate attributed claims with their evidence. Explain why one is preferred when there is a defensible reason. Do not use equal visual weight to imply equally strong evidence. Do not erase older interpretations: mark them superseded, challenged or unresolved and show the link.

## Narrative and silence

A topic can have rich oral traditions but sparse accessible written sources. A digital corpus can privilege officials, elites or colonial observers. Catalogue absence, failure to retrieve a document and failure to find a claim in a searched section are different from proof that no record exists. Document the search scope before making any negative claim.

Year files without entries are open work queues. They should never say “nothing happened.” Period-level uncertainty is part of the history, not a defect to conceal with fluent filler.

## AI and human review

Automated tools may assist discovery, deduplication, OCR triage and draft paraphrase. They may not silently publish extracted claims as approved history. Preserve extraction provenance and mark machine work. Human reviewers should compare important claims with the actual sources, check the dating and geography, and disclose the limits of their expertise.
